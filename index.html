Standing Water in Your Catch Basin? Here's What It's Telling You

A catch basin is supposed to do one thing: take water in and send it downstream. So when you lift the grate and find standing water in the catch basin days after the last rain, the basin is filing a complaint. It's telling you something in the system has failed — and the longer that water sits, the more expensive the underlying problem becomes.

Cause One: Sediment Has Eaten the Sump

Every catch basin has a sump — the deep section below the outlet pipe that traps sediment before it can clog the downstream line. That sump has a finite capacity, and in Bellevue's leaf-heavy fall, it fills faster than most property owners expect. Once sediment reaches the outlet level, water can't drain fully; it ponds at the sediment line. This is the most common cause we find, and the fix is straightforward: vacuum the basin down to its original depth. The before-and-after difference is usually dramatic — several inches of recovered capacity from a basin that "looked fine" from the surface.

Cause Two: The Outlet Line Is Blocked

If the basin is clean and water still stands, the problem is downstream. The outlet pipe may be crushed, root-invaded, or packed with sediment that migrated past a full sump. This needs a camera or a jetting run to diagnose — you're looking at the connecting line, not the basin itself. We see this often in older commercial properties where the original clay or concrete storm lines have deteriorated. The basin is just the messenger; the blockage is twenty feet away underground.

Cause Three: Grade and Settlement

Sometimes the basin was installed wrong, or the ground settled around it. If the outlet pipe sits higher than the basin floor by design — or if settlement tilted the whole structure — water will always pond below the outlet level. Minor settlement can sometimes be corrected by regrading around the basin; a badly installed or severely settled basin may need to be reset. This is less common than sediment, but when we find it, cleaning alone will never fix it, and we say so upfront rather than selling repeat cleanings.

Why Standing Water Can't Wait

Three risks make this urgent. First, mosquitoes: standing water is a breeding ground, and a neglected basin can produce thousands per week in summer — your tenants and neighbors will notice. Second, freeze damage: in winter, standing water freezes, expands, and cracks basin walls and outlet pipes, turning a cleaning into a structural repair. Third, capacity: every inch of standing water is an inch of storm capacity you've lost, which means the next big rain overwhelms the system faster and floods further. A basin with chronic standing water is a flooding incident waiting for a storm.

The Fix, Step by Step

Our approach: inspect the basin and measure the standing depth, vacuum out all sediment and debris to restore the original sump, run water to verify it drains to the outlet level, and if it doesn't, camera-inspect the outlet line to find the downstream restriction. Then we document everything — depths, volumes removed, line condition — so you have a baseline for next time. Most basins go from problem to solved in a single visit. The ones that don't tell us something important about the underground system, and that's exactly the kind of thing you want to know before the rainy season, not during it.

How Often Should Basins Be Serviced?

The honest answer: it depends on the basin's surroundings, but most Bellevue catch basins need attention at least once a year, and many need it twice. A basin under mature firs fills with needles three times faster than one in an open lot. Commercial properties with heavy traffic track in sediment constantly. The right approach is to establish a baseline — full cleanout, documented sump depth — then check back in six months and measure how much has accumulated. That one measurement tells you your interval: if the sump is half full in six months, you're on a twice-yearly schedule; if it's barely dusted, annual is fine. Properties that skip this and guess usually guess wrong in the expensive direction. Put the basins on a calendar like any other maintenance, and the standing-water problem never gets a chance to start.
